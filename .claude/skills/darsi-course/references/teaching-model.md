# The teaching model, and why

One structure for every video and every lesson. It is fixed so that learners always know where they are, authors
never start from a blank page, and quality does not depend on who wrote the lesson.

## Where it comes from

The structure is Merrill's First Principles of Instruction, fitted to a three-minute phone video and the lesson
around it. Merrill compared many instructional design theories and found five things they agree on. Learning is
promoted when:

1. learners work on a **real problem** (problem-centred);
2. what they already know is **activated** as a base for the new;
3. the new thing is **demonstrated**, not only told;
4. learners **apply** it themselves and get feedback;
5. they **integrate** it into their own world.

Each part of a Darsi lesson does one of these jobs:

| Merrill | In the video | In the lesson around it |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | Hook | The project is the same kind of problem, in the learner's school |
| Activation | Show it (an everyday example they already know) | The reading opens on the hook's problem |
| Demonstration | Name it, Work it, Widen it | The reading, with the same worked example |
| Application | Check 1, Check 2 | Practice questions, the lab, the unit check |
| Integration | Try it tomorrow | Reflection, project, kit |

The order inside the video also follows Gagné's events of instruction (gain attention, state the objective, recall
what is known, present, guide, elicit performance, give feedback, help retention and transfer), which is why the
promise comes straight after the hook and every check is followed by feedback that says why.

## The rules and the reason for each

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Open with a hook, and leave it open.** | A question the learner can't yet answer makes them want the answer (the information gap: Loewenstein). A hook that answers itself closes the gap before the lesson starts. |
| **Promise one thing they will be able to do.** | A stated outcome tells learners what to attend to, and it is what you test (backward design: Wiggins and McTighe). "Today: X and Y" names a topic, not an ability. |
| **Start from what they already know.** | What a learner already knows is the largest single influence on what they learn next (Ausubel). A beginner has no knowledge of the subject, so the base must be everyday life: the register, the market, a phone. |
| **Example first, then the name.** | Beginners learn an abstract idea better from a concrete case that is then named and generalised (concreteness fading: Bruner; Fyfe and others). A definition first is words attached to nothing. |
| **One idea per scene, at most four new terms per video.** | Working memory holds about four new things at once (Cowan). Short segments with one idea each are learned better than a continuous stream (Mayer's segmenting principle). |
| **Work one case through, step by step.** | Novices learn more from studying a worked example than from solving the same problem cold (the worked-example effect: Sweller and Cooper). |
| **Checks ask for use, not repetition, and explain why.** | Retrieving and using an idea strengthens it far more than hearing it again (the testing effect: Roediger and Karpicke). Feedback helps when it says why (Hattie and Timperley). |
| **Say what it is not.** | Non-examples and near misses mark the edge of a concept, and they are where misconceptions live. |
| **Few words on screen; items arrive as they are said.** | People learn better from pictures with spoken words than with the same words written out (Mayer's modality and redundancy principles), and when a picture and its words come together (contiguity). Cut what doesn't serve the outcome (coherence). |
| **Talk to the learner as "you", like a colleague.** | Conversational style is learned better than formal style (Mayer's personalisation principle). |
| **Three minutes.** | In large studies of online course videos, engagement falls quickly after about six minutes, and shorter is better (Guo, Kim and Rubin). Three minutes also keeps a lesson near 400 KB per language, which matters on paid data. |
| **Return to the hook, recap in three points, name the next lesson.** | Closing the opening question shows the learner what they gained. A short recap and a pointer forward help retention and make the course feel like a path, not a pile. |
| **Come back to earlier ideas later.** | Practice spread over time lasts longer than practice bunched together (spacing: Cepeda and others). The unit check and the next lesson's "Show it" reuse earlier ideas on purpose. |

## What "start from zero" means

- The first lesson of a track's first course may assume only everyday life and what the onboarding already asked for
  (the learner can read, use a phone, and teaches or studies at a school).
- It may not assume another Darsi track. If lesson 1 of AI agents needs the idea "a chatbot guesses likely words", it
  teaches that in a sentence or two, with an example. It does not say "as you learned in AI basics".
- From the second lesson on, a lesson may use anything an earlier lesson **in the same path** taught, and should say
  so ("Last time, the agent called a tool…").
- A course above Beginner states what it expects, in one line at the end of its summary: "Before this: …". Even then,
  each idea it borrows gets a one-sentence reminder the first time it is used.
- A word is "taught" only when it was said, shown on screen, and used in an example. A word that only appeared in a
  list of key terms is not yet taught.

## Levels are a ladder of what learners can do

The four levels follow Bloom's revised taxonomy, so each course's outcomes use verbs from its own level.

| Level | Teachers | Outcome verbs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Beginner | Knows what it is, where they meet it, how to use it safely | recognise, name, tell apart, explain in your own words, use safely |
| 2 Elementary | Can explain how it works inside, and has tried it by hand | explain how, trace, predict, try, change |
| 3 Intermediate | Applies it to real school problems and judges the results | apply, build, test, compare, judge |
| 4 Advanced | Designs with it, critiques it, leads others | design, evaluate, critique, write a policy, teach others |

For students, the four levels are school stages (primary 9 to 12, lower secondary 12 to 15, upper secondary 15 to 18,
university). `references/course.md` says how the writing changes with each.

## When you want to break the structure

Don't change the order or drop a part. What you may vary is inside a part: which of the three hook kinds, how many
"show it" scenes (one or two), how many "work it" scenes (one to three), whether "widen it" has a non-example. If a
topic truly cannot fit, it is two lessons, not one long one.
